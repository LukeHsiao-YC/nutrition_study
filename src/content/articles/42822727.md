### 重點摘要
這項研究旨在探討Go/No-Go (GNG) 訓練對食物評價的影響機制，特別是預先給予的刺激反應指令是否能立即改變食物喜好。兩項研究比較了詳細指令與一般指令對熟悉及不熟悉食物在訓練前後評價的影響。結果顯示，預先指令本身並未在訓練前改變食物評價；然而，在GNG訓練後，「Go」相關食物的評價普遍提升，而熟悉食物的「No-Go」評價則下降，不熟悉食物則未見普遍下降。研究結論指出，食物評價的改變可能更依賴於訓練過程中的實際體驗和詮釋，而非單純的預先指令。

### 研究縫隙 (Research Gap)
本研究釐清了Go/No-Go訓練中，預先給予的刺激反應指令並不能立即影響食物評價，而是訓練期間的實際經驗與詮釋才是評價改變的關鍵，填補了指令在行為訓練效果中扮演角色的知識空白。未來的研究應更深入探討在任務執行過程中，受試者如何體驗和詮釋刺激反應，以及這種機制如何導致評價變化，特別是對於不熟悉食物的去價值化效應，仍需進一步驗證與探索其潛在影響因素（如特質衝動性）。

### 原文資訊 (Original English)
**Title:** Can liking be instructed? Evaluations of familiar and unfamiliar food following Go/No-Go instructions and training.
**Abstract:** Go/No-Go (GNG) training can influence food evaluations, yet the processes underlying these effects remain debated. Associative accounts propose that evaluative change emerges through repeated stimulus-response learning, whereas inferential accounts emphasise the evaluative meaning individuals derive from stimulus-response relations. This research examined whether instructions about stimulus-response contingencies can influence food evaluations before the corresponding responses are performed. Across two preregistered studies, participants received either detailed contingency instructions or general task instructions. In both conditions, go responses were framed as selecting a food and no-go responses as rejecting a food. The detailed instructions additionally specified which food category was associated with each response. Detailed instructions also framed go responses as selecting a food and no-go responses as rejecting a food. Food evaluations were assessed after instruction but before training (T1), and after GNG training (T2). Study 1 (n = 54) used familiar food stimuli, whereas Study 2 (n = 63) used novel, unfamiliar food stimuli. At T1, evaluations of go- and no-go-associated foods did not differ between instruction conditions in either study. Following GNG training, go-associated stimuli were evaluated more positively in both studies, whereas no-go devaluation was observed for familiar foods in Study 1 but not overall for unfamiliar foods in Study 2. Exploratory analyses in Study 2 indicated stronger no-go devaluation among participants higher in trait impulsivity. These findings suggest that providing information about stimulus-response contingencies before training was not reflected in differences in food evaluations. The observed changes in evaluations following training suggest that the evaluative impact of these stimulus-response assignments may depend in part on how responses are experienced and interpreted during task performance, although this possibility requires further investigation.
