### 重點摘要
本研究旨在探討「go/no-go」訓練如何影響女性對食物的渴望及其相關的大腦活動。研究招募85名女性，訓練她們對特定食物做出反應（go foods）或抑制反應（no-go foods），並在訓練前後評估食物渴望度及利用功能性磁振造影（fMRI）觀察腦部活動。結果顯示，「no-go」食物的渴望度在訓練後顯著下降，且此去價值化效應與額中、枕中及枕下迴的血氧依賴性反應降低有關。研究結論指出，單純的行為反應或不反應即可改變主觀食物評價，且這些評價變化可能與訓練後的即時食物選擇相關。

### 研究縫隙 (Research Gap)
這項研究透過行為與腦部影像分析，揭示了go/no-go訓練如何改變女性對食物的渴望及其相關的大腦機制，為理解行為介入對食物評價的神經基礎提供了新見解。然而，研究中的中介分析因在同次會期中進行，未能確立明確的時間或因果關係，且僅針對女性樣本，其結果推廣至其他人群的普適性有待未來研究探證。

### 原文資訊 (Original English)
**Title:** Food go/no-go training changes food wanting in women: Behavioral, brain activity, and choice outcomes.
**Abstract:** Food evaluations can be altered by associating mere action versus inaction with these foods. In this study, we examined how food go/no-go training influences food wanting and its brain correlates using functional magnetic resonance imaging in a sample of women (N = 85). During the training session, participants learned to consistently respond to go foods while suppressing responses to no-go foods. Food wanting was assessed through a rating task in which participants reported how much they wanted to eat the go and no-go foods before and after training. The results revealed that wanting ratings for no-go foods declined significantly after training. Functional magnetic resonance imaging data indicated that the devaluation effect was associated with lower blood-oxygen-level-dependent responses in the middle frontal, middle occipital, and inferior occipital gyri. Exploratory dynamic causal modelling was used to characterize post-training modulation of effective connectivity among these three regions, with the strongest effects involving bidirectional middle frontal gyrus-inferior occipital gyrus connectivity. Statistical mediation analysis suggested an indirect association between the inferior occipital gyrus-to-middle frontal gyrus modulatory effect and post-training food choice through food wanting, although this same-session analysis involved closely related preference readouts and does not establish temporal or causal mediation. Overall, the findings suggest that simply responding or not responding to certain foods can alter subjective food evaluation, and that these evaluation changes may be related to immediate post-training food choice.
