### 重點摘要
亞洲國家因人口老化和生活方式轉變，面臨飲食相關非傳染性疾病的沉重負擔，儘管傳統亞洲飲食有其優點，但精緻碳水化合物和高鈉攝取是其限制，且現今飲食模式轉變加劇了挑戰。地中海飲食雖有益，但不完全適用於亞洲文化。因此，本研究提出「亞洲地中海飲食 (AMED)」作為一種文化適應框架，整合地中海飲食的原則與亞洲健康食物和烹飪方式，強調植物性蛋白、全穀物、蔬果、海鮮、禽肉及傳統功能性食品。目前一項為期20週的隨機對照試驗正在新加坡進行中，旨在評估AMED的可行性、可接受性及健康效益。研究結論指出，AMED有望成為一種可推廣的策略，結合地中海飲食的實證基礎與亞洲飲食文化，促進亞洲人口健康長壽。

### 研究縫隙 (Research Gap)
這篇文獻填補了亞洲地區缺乏統一、文化適宜且有實證基礎的健康飲食模式的研究空白，特別是針對地中海飲食難以直接應用於亞洲文化的限制。未來的研究限制在於其長期健康效益及在不同亞洲人群中的廣泛可擴展性，仍需更多證據來累積與驗證。

### 原文資訊 (Original English)
**Title:** Towards an Optimal Diet for Health in Asia: The Asian-Mediterranean Dietary Pattern.
**Abstract:** With rapid population aging and lifestyle transitions, Asian countries are facing a growing burden of diet-related non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Optimal nutrition is central to preventing NCDs and promoting healthy aging. Traditional Asian diets have many strengths, including abundant vegetables, plant-based proteins, functional foods, and nutrient-preserving cooking methods such as steaming and stewing. However, they also have limitations, including widespread use of refined carbohydrates and high sodium intake. In recent decades, the nutrition transition towards energy-dense, highly processed diets has further exacerbated these existing challenges and contributed to declining dietary quality. Despite efforts across Asia to establish healthy dietary frameworks, the region's diverse food cultures and limited research make it challenging to conceptualize a unified, culturally appropriate, and evidence-based dietary pattern. The Mediterranean diet has gained global recognition for its health benefits. However, its direct application in Asia is limited by cultural, culinary, and food system differences. In this Perspective, the Asian-Mediterranean Diet (AMED) is proposed as a culturally adapted framework that preserves the evidence-based principles of the Mediterranean diet while integrating healthy Asian foods and culinary practices. It emphasizes plant-based proteins such as soy products, healthy plant oils like canola and sesame oils, whole grains including brown rice and millet, abundant seasonal fruits and vegetables, seafood and poultry, and traditional functional foods such as fermented foods and teas. Nutrient-preserving cooking methods, together with moderate use of condiments, are encouraged. A 20-week randomized controlled trial in Singapore is underway to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, adherence, and health effects of AMED. As evidence accumulates, AMED has the potential to provide a scalable strategy that bridges Mediterranean evidence with Asian food cultures, promoting healthier and longer lives across Asian populations.
